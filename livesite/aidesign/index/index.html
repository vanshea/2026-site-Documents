Self Care

A lock-screen self-care snack bar for quick mood check-ins. Name how you feel, note the small daily factors around you, and log a tiny check-in without opening a full app.

Self Care — The Problem

Self-care is consistently the first commitment people deprioritize — not from lack of intent, but because of decision fatigue: the well-documented depletion of willpower and judgment that accumulates across a day of choices. By evening, the mental resources needed to choose a healthy meal, go for a walk, or simply rest are the same resources already spent all day on everyone else's needs. The result is a predictable, almost mechanical pattern of self-neglect — not a personal failing, but a resource-allocation problem.

Self Care addresses this by lowering the decision cost of caring for yourself: small, well-timed nudges and low-friction choices offered at the moments willpower is already thin, rather than demanding fresh discipline on top of an already-depleted reserve. Less self-negotiation, less guilt, more room for care to actually happen.

Sources: Baumeister, R. F. et al. (1998), "Ego Depletion: Is the Active Self a Limited Resource?", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology; Vohs, K. D. et al. (2005), "Decision Fatigue Exhausts Self-Regulatory Resources"; Tierney, J. (2011), "Do You Suffer From Decision Fatigue?", The New York Times Magazine.

Meeting Coach

A live lock-screen meeting coach that scrolls the conversation, tracks predetermined talking points and expertise, scores communication clarity, and separately signals whether the room is going well, mixed, or poorly.

Meeting Coach — The Problem

Knowledge workers spend an average of 18-23 hours a week in meetings, yet studies on "meeting recovery syndrome" show that poorly run meetings leave a residue of fatigue and unresolved tension that follows people into their next task. The core issue isn't meeting quantity — it's the absence of structure around preparation, real-time facilitation, and follow-through. Without a clear thread connecting what was decided, who owns what, and what changed as a result, meetings become isolated events rather than links in a chain of progress. This creates a quiet but corrosive form of organizational memory loss: decisions get re-litigated, action items evaporate, and people leave meetings uncertain whether their time was well spent.

Meeting Coach reduces this by acting as a steady hand across the full meeting lifecycle — helping people walk in prepared, stay oriented to purpose while they're in it, and leave with clarity on what happens next. Less re-explaining, less redundant discussion, more confidence that each meeting actually moved something forward.

Sources: Allen, J. A., Mroz, J. et al., University of Nebraska Omaha, research on meeting recovery syndrome; "Why am I so exhausted?: Exploring Meeting-to-Work Transition Time and Recovery from Virtual Meeting Fatigue," PMC/National Institutes of Health.

Contact

A people-first contact hub that brings conversations across channels into one prioritized view, surfaces who is waiting on you, and helps you prepare for the people in your next meeting.

Contact — The Problem

The average knowledge worker checks 6-10 communication apps daily — Messages, Mail, Slack, WhatsApp, Teams, Phone — each with its own inbox, notification style, and mental model. Every app-switch carries a hidden tax: cognitive psychologists call it task-switching cost, the residual attention left behind each time your brain re-orients to a new context. Multiply that across dozens of daily switches, and the result isn't just lost time — it's cumulative cognitive load, the working-memory strain of holding open threads across fragmented systems.

The deeper issue is that these tools organize around channels (which app) and objectives (which meeting, which task) — not around the people we're actually accountable to. So we lose the thread on who's waiting, who matters most today, and what they actually need, buried under a pile of undifferentiated notifications.

Contact reduces this load by collapsing every channel into one relationship-centered layer — prioritizing people over platforms, so your attention goes to who needs you, not which app pinged you. Less switching, less noise, more presence with the people who matter.

Sources: Leroy, S. (2009), "Why is it so hard to do my work?", Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes — origin of "attention residue"; Mark, G. et al., UC Irvine research on interruption recovery time; American Psychological Association, research on multitasking and switch costs.
