Curriculum Strategy

Information Architecture was reframed as part of a larger human-centered process.

The Conflict

At NYU, students were learning Information Architecture at a moment when the UX field was rapidly changing. Traditional IA methods still mattered, but students were at risk of learning structure without enough exposure to user behavior, research, testing, and evidence-based decision-making.

They could create site maps and navigation systems, but many had not yet learned how to validate whether those structures made sense to real people.

The Strategic Action

Van recognized that the curriculum needed to meet the reality of the industry. He expanded the course beyond static organization and classification methods, introducing students to the broader discipline of user experience design.

He reframed Information Architecture as part of a larger human-centered process. Students learned to consider user goals, behavioral patterns, research findings, usability principles, and real-world context. The classroom became a bridge between academic design training and professional UX practice.

Impact Beyond the Classroom

  • Student Win: Helped students understand that good design begins with evidence, not personal preference or internal logic.
  • Professional Win: Created a UX-centered learning experience for 60+ students annually, influencing the career development of roughly 300 designers over time.
  • Industry Win: Prepared students for the actual expectations of modern design teams, where research, testing, iteration, and cross-functional collaboration are core skills.
  • Strategic Win: Shifted the educational focus from “structured site maps” to “evidence-based design decisions.”
  • Teaching Win: Turned curriculum design into a service design problem: who are the students, what do they need to become capable of, and why does that matter in the professional world they are entering?

Strategic Takeaway

Van helped students move from organizing information to understanding people. The larger contribution was not simply teaching a class. It was updating the learning model so emerging designers could enter the field with a stronger foundation in research, empathy, and evidence-based practice.
